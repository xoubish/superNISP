\abstract{
\Euclid's near-infrared NISP imaging reaches galaxy populations relevant to weak lensing, but its coarse sampling limits measurements of galaxy structure.
We investigate whether learned super-resolution improves image fidelity and observed shapes while preserving sensitivity to applied shear.
We train a conditional diffusion model and two residual-network variants on 17\,816 NISP \YE-band cutouts paired with JWST/NIRCam F115W images of the COSMOS field, and evaluate them on 2118 validation galaxies.
The residual networks achieve lower reconstruction errors and stronger spatial correlation with JWST than diffusion, whereas diffusion retains more observed small-scale power.
Relative to native NISP, the residual network reduces the ellipticity-component RMSE against JWST by approximately 19\% with fixed weighted moments and 11\% with adaptive moments.
Improvements are largest at high signal-to-noise; at low signal-to-noise only a subset of galaxies yields measurable shapes.
Reconstruction errors are consistent across three training seeds.
Controlled simulations using the NISP effective point-spread functions show increased shear response for compact galaxies after reconstruction, but the gain depends on size and noise, and responses remain below the intrinsic value.
These results support residual networks as a promising route to improving observed-shape fidelity in NISP imaging.
Establishing calibrated shear accuracy will require realistic simulations and independent validation that account for noise, instrumental response, cross-band differences, and measurement selection.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sc:intro}

Weak gravitational lensing is one of the primary cosmological probes of
Stage IV wide-field surveys \citep{Laureijs2011, Chang2013}, and its
statistical precision places demanding requirements on the control of
systematic errors. Shape measurement in particular has required a
sustained, community-wide effort: successive blind challenges
\citep{Heymans2006, Massey2007, Mandelbaum2015} and calibration
frameworks based on image simulations and self-calibration
\citep[e.g.,][]{Huff2017, Sheldon2017, EC_Congedo2024} have been devoted
to measuring and removing biases in shape estimators. For \Euclid,
multiplicative shear biases must be controlled to $\sim$$10^{-3}$
\citep{Cropper2013}, so any new shape-measurement technique must be
tested for bias and calibrated before its outputs can support
cosmological inference.

Deep learning has meanwhile become integral to astronomical data
analysis, with applications ranging from image generation and outlier
detection to photometric redshifts and deblending
\citep{Arcelin2021, Hemmati2022, Sampson2024, Scognamiglio2025}.
Super-resolution, the task of inferring high-resolution spatial
information from lower-resolution inputs \citep{Anwar2019}, has been
applied from radio interferometry to solar and ground-based optical
imaging \citep[e.g.,][]{Dabbech_2022, Song2024, Sukurdeep2025}. In
\Euclid, weak-lensing shapes are measured in the visible band with VIS
\citep[$0\farcs1$ pixels;][]{EuclidSkyVIS}, while the near-infrared
imaging from NISP \citep[$0\farcs3$ pixels;][]{EuclidSkyNISP} is three
times coarser in sampling and is not used for shape measurement.
Galaxies that are well detected in the near-infrared but lack usable
VIS shapes, including red and high-redshift sources, are therefore
excluded from the weak-lensing sample. Studies with HST/CANDELS imaging
have demonstrated the potential of near-infrared shapes: \citet{Lee2018}
compared ellipticities in optical and near-infrared images degraded to
survey quality, and \citet{Finner2023} examined WFC3-IR PSF modelling
and weak-lensing systematics. Super-resolved NISP images could extend
the range of galaxies with useful shapes, increasing the source density
and redshift coverage available to lensing analyses.

This paper is a first step towards that goal. We train deep-learning
super-resolution models on \Euclid\ NISP images of the COSMOS field and
evaluate them against overlapping JWST/NIRCam imaging, which provides a
factor of five finer sampling and an independent reference for every
galaxy (Sect.~\ref{sc:observations}). We compare a conditional diffusion
model \citep{sohldickstein2015, Ho2020}, which generates images through
successive denoising steps, with a residual network built from residual
dense blocks \citep{Wang2018}, which predicts an image in a single pass
(Sect.~\ref{sc:methods}). We evaluate both on image fidelity, spatial
structure, and observed ellipticity, examine how shape errors depend on
input signal-to-noise, and test the response of the residual network to
an applied shear in controlled simulations (Sect.~\ref{sc:results}).
Our sample is limited to optically selected galaxies with $F814W<25$, so
the results establish the method on a well-characterized population
rather than on the fainter sources that motivate the application; we
return to this in Sect.~\ref{sc:discussion}.

\section{\label{sc:observations}Observations}

We use \Euclid\ NISP \YE-band imaging \citep{EuclidSkyNISP} and overlapping JWST/NIRCam F115W imaging \citep{jwst_nircam} in the COSMOS field \citep{Scoville_2007}.
The NISP images are co-added Quick Release Q1 products \citep{Q1cite}, processed by the NIR pipeline at approximately $0\farcs3$ per pixel \citep{Q1-TP003}.
The JWST images are the COSMOS-Web F115W mosaics \citep{cosmos_web_overview} at $0\farcs06$ per pixel.
The \YE\ and F115W passbands overlap but are not identical, so differences in stellar populations and dust contribute to differences between the images.

We extract $41\times41$ NISP cutouts and construct $205\times205$ target images over the same approximately $12\arcsec$ field, sampling the sky five times more finely than the NISP input.
This change in sampling does not by itself define the recovered resolution: the targets retain the JWST point-spread function (PSF), mosaic resampling, and the interpolation described in Sect.~\ref{sc:preprocessing}.
We therefore evaluate reconstructions against the observed F115W images rather than against a deconvolved truth.

We select COSMOS2020 Classic galaxy-class objects \citep[\texttt{lp\_type}$=0$;][]{cosmos2020_classic} with ACS F814W AB magnitude $<25$, EAZY photometric redshift $0.01<z_{\rm phot}<3$, and $4<\texttt{FLUX\_RADIUS}<500$ catalogue pixels, where the radius encloses half of the flux in the PSF-homogenized $K_s$ image.
The magnitude threshold follows the approximate $riz<25$ Euclid-depth selection of \citet[Sect.~5.2]{shooby_calibration}, with F814W as an optical-band proxy.
These cuts define a sample for resolved-morphology analysis; they do not implement a survey-specific shape-quality selection.

The inputs comprise four NISP \YE-band stacks and 19 F115W mosaics, of which all four stacks and 18 mosaics contribute accepted galaxies.
Starting from 39\,024 candidates from the COSMOS2020 catalogue, the extraction retains 22\,810 paired galaxies after coverage, pixel-quality, and spatial-buffer cuts.
We assign disjoint $3\arcmin$ sky blocks to training, validation, and test subsets, excluding galaxies within $10\arcsec$ of block boundaries, which gives 17\,816 training, 2118 validation, and 2876 test galaxies.
Training data fit the model weights and normalization scales; validation data support checkpoint selection and development of the models and measurement protocol.
All comparisons of observed galaxies in this paper use the validation sample.
The test sample is reserved for an independent evaluation of the final procedure.
Figure~\ref{fig:sample_overview} shows the spatial distribution and an example pair; Fig.~\ref{fig:sample_properties} compares the magnitude and redshift distributions of the three subsets with the parent catalogue.

\section{\label{sc:methods}Methods}

We compare a conditional diffusion model and two variants of a residual convolutional network for reconstructing JWST F115W images from NISP \YE-band observations.
The diffusion model generates an image through repeated updates starting from random noise; the residual networks predict an image in a single pass.
All models learn from the paired training images, so their outputs depend on both the NISP measurements and the galaxy population in the training sample.

Throughout, the reference is the \emph{observed} F115W image.
It retains the JWST PSF, instrumental noise, resampling, and the \YE--F115W band difference, and we apply no PSF deconvolution or band correction.
Agreement with this reference therefore measures fidelity to the observed JWST image, not intrinsic galaxy shape or calibrated gravitational shear.
We state this once here; the shape statistics in Sect.~\ref{sc:results} should all be read in this sense.

\subsection{Image preparation}
\label{sc:preprocessing}

We construct paired cutouts using the COSMOS2020 coordinates and the world coordinate system (WCS) of each mosaic.
The NISP cutout contains $41\times41$ native pixels, extracted with \texttt{Astropy} \citep{astropy_latest} without rotation or interpolation.
We define a $205\times205$ output grid with the same footprint and orientation, subdividing each NISP pixel into $5\times5$ target pixels of approximately $0\farcs06$.
Each target pixel is mapped through both WCS solutions to the JWST mosaic and its surface brightness is obtained by bilinear interpolation \citep{scipy}.
We apply no additional shifts fitted from the galaxy images, so residual survey astrometric errors can contribute to reconstruction differences.

A NISP pixel is valid when its science value is finite, its data-quality flag is zero, and its supplied root-mean-square (RMS) uncertainty is finite and positive.
The JWST mosaics lack quality and uncertainty maps in this dataset, so we use finite, nonzero pixels as a coverage proxy.
An interpolated target pixel is valid only when all contributing source pixels are valid.
We require at least 95\% valid pixels in each cutout and complete coverage of the central $5\times5$ NISP pixels.

We estimate and subtract a constant background per image from the sigma-clipped median of the outer pixels, excluding a central square of roughly half the cutout width.
NISP fluxes are converted to MJy\,sr$^{-1}$ using the local pixel solid angle; JWST images are already in these units.
Each band is then divided by a fixed scale determined from the training set alone: the median NISP pixel RMS (0.02307\,MJy\,sr$^{-1}$) and the median JWST outer-region standard deviation (0.01365\,MJy\,sr$^{-1}$).
Individual galaxies are not normalized by their own flux.
Invalid pixels are set to zero after scaling, and negative intensities are retained.
All physical image and shape comparisons use background-subtracted linear intensities with the transformations inverted and no clipping.
Training losses exclude invalid target pixels and give each galaxy equal weight.

\subsection{Diffusion model}
\label{sc:diffusion_model}

The diffusion model follows the image-refinement approach of \citet{SR3}, based on denoising diffusion models \citep{Ho2020, Nichol2021}.
During training, we add a known amount of Gaussian noise to a JWST cutout and train the network to recover the original image.
Repeating this at many noise levels teaches the model to proceed from a random field towards an F115W reconstruction (Fig.~\ref{fig:diff_model}).

The NISP image is placed on the output grid by bilinear interpolation as a spatially aligned conditioning input, with a second map identifying valid NISP pixels so that the network can distinguish missing data from faint emission.
Both bands are passed through $\mathrm{asinh}(I/3)$, where $I$ is the scaled, background-subtracted intensity.
This reversible transformation compresses bright intensities while retaining negative values; it is inverted before evaluation.

The network is a U-Net whose encoder levels contain 32, 64, and 128 feature maps, with a central stage of 128 and a symmetric decoder.
Skip connections pass encoder features to the corresponding decoder level.
Each stage contains two residual blocks.
The NISP image, its validity map, and the current noise level modulate the internal features through learned rescalings and offsets (FiLM; \citealt{Perez2017}).
Images are padded from 205 to 208 pixels internally and cropped on output.

For each training pair we sample one of 1000 noise levels from a cosine schedule.
Writing the transformed target as $x_0$ and a standard Gaussian field as $\epsilon$, the noisy image and the predicted quantity are
\begin{equation}
    x_t=a_t x_0+b_t\epsilon, \qquad v_t=a_t\epsilon-b_t x_0,
\end{equation}
where $a_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$, $b_t=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$, and $\bar\alpha_t$ is the fraction of signal variance retained at step $t$.
From the prediction $\hat v_t$, the reconstructed target is $\hat x_0=a_t x_t-b_t\hat v_t$.
We minimize
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\rm diff}
    =\left\langle(\hat v_t-v_t)^2\right\rangle_V
    +0.1\left\langle|\hat x_0-x_0|\right\rangle_V,
\end{equation}
where $\langle\cdot\rangle_V$ averages over valid JWST pixels within each galaxy and then over the batch, in the transformed coordinates.

To generate an image, we start from a Gaussian field and apply 50 denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM) updates \citep{Song2021DDIM} conditioned on the NISP image and mask.
No further noise is added during generation, so a fixed initial field gives a reproducible reconstruction, while a different initial field gives a different one.

\subsection{Residual networks}
\label{sc:residual_model}

The residual network produces an F115W estimate in a single pass.
It first extracts features on the $41\times41$ grid, then enlarges them to the $205\times205$ output.
We compare two variants with the same feature extraction and different enlargement paths: a two-stage network (ResNet) and a direct-$5\times$ network (ResNet-D5; Fig.~\ref{fig:resnet_model}), each trained separately.

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/residual_architecture_v06.pdf}
    \caption{Residual networks.
    (a) Shared feature extraction: eight residual dense blocks, edge filtering, and channel weighting.
    (b) Enlargement paths: ResNet enlarges twice by $2\times$ to $164\times164$ and resizes to $205\times205$; ResNet-D5 enlarges directly by $5\times$.
    (c) A residual dense block; plus symbols denote addition.}
    \label{fig:resnet_model}
\end{figure*}

Feature extraction combines residual learning \citep{ResNet} with the dense reuse of intermediate features used in image super-resolution \citep{Wang2018}.
An initial convolution produces 64 feature maps, which pass through eight residual dense blocks.
Within each block, four convolutions each add 32 feature maps to the accumulated input, a fifth combines them into 64 maps, and the result is added to the block input.
After the eight blocks, a convolution combines the features and a long skip adds back the initial maps.
A further module applies a fixed edge-detection filter to each feature map, adds the response to the features, and processes the result with learned convolutions; channel weights computed from each map's mean response \citep{hu2019} then rescale the maps before enlargement.

Both enlargement paths use pixel shuffling, in which a convolution predicts extra feature maps that are rearranged onto a finer grid \citep{Shi2016PixelShuffle}.
ResNet doubles the grid twice, from 41 to 82 to 164 pixels, predicts an image, and resizes it bilinearly to $205\times205$.
ResNet-D5 enlarges in one step from 41 to 205 pixels and predicts directly on the target grid.
ResNet-D5 also has more parameters (Table~\ref{tab:training_settings}), so the comparison includes a change in capacity.

Both variants take the linearly scaled NISP image as their only input.
Each is trained with the mean absolute difference between prediction and JWST target over valid pixels.
This objective acts on linear intensity, whereas the diffusion loss acts in asinh coordinates.

\subsection{Training and model selection}
\label{sc:training}

All three models use the same 17\,816 training galaxies and a budget of 50 epochs at batch size eight (111\,350 updates).
We apply random $90\degr$ rotations and reflections consistently to each paired batch; validation images are not augmented.
Training uses Adam with a one-cycle learning-rate schedule peaking at $10^{-4}$.
Table~\ref{tab:training_settings} summarizes the settings.
The equal update budget does not imply equal computation, since the models differ in parameter count, objective, and generation cost.

We select the checkpoint with the lowest validation mean absolute image error.
For the residual networks this uses all 2118 validation galaxies.
For diffusion, where generation requires 50 network evaluations per galaxy, we monitor a fixed random subset of 256 validation galaxies with fixed initial noise fields.
Shape measurements are not used for checkpoint selection.
The selected checkpoints are epoch 35 for diffusion and epoch 49 for both residual networks.

To assess variation with the initial noise, we generate three independently seeded diffusion images per validation galaxy and also evaluate their mean in linear surface brightness as a separate reconstruction.
To assess variation between training runs, we repeat the full ResNet training with two additional seeds, keeping everything else fixed; the original checkpoint remains the main model regardless of outcome.
We also tested ten-epoch continuations of ResNet with a reweighted loss and with PSF- and noise-aware inputs.
These produced no clear improvement and are described in Appendix~\ref{ap:continuations}.

\subsection{Image and shape comparisons}
\label{sc:measurement}

We compare each reconstruction with the JWST reference, the native NISP image, and bilinear and bicubic enlargements of NISP on the target grid.
Interpolation uses background-subtracted linear intensities.

\subsubsection{Regional errors and spatial structure}
\label{sc:regional_structure}

We measure reconstruction errors in the central $105\times105$ target pixels (approximately $6\farcs3$) of all 2118 validation objects, separately within and outside source regions.
Source masks are defined from the JWST reference and shared by all methods: we smooth the reference with a one-pixel Gaussian, detect connected regions of at least ten pixels above two standard deviations of the sky, fill holes, and dilate by three pixels.
Errors are the per-object RMSE in signed, background-subtracted intensity, averaged over galaxies with equal weight.

To separate the amount of spatial variation from its agreement with the reference, we measure the annular Fourier power ratio and the signed correlation
\begin{equation}
 r(k)=\frac{\mathrm{Re}\langle F_{\rm model} F_{\rm JWST}^{*}\rangle_k}
 {\sqrt{\langle |F_{\rm model}|^2\rangle_k\langle |F_{\rm JWST}|^2\rangle_k}}
\end{equation}
within each region, after mean removal and tapering with a common window.
We report the geometric mean of per-object power ratios and the arithmetic mean of per-object correlations over the 1363 completely valid cutouts.
The reference spectra include noise, and no noise subtraction, PSF correction, or window deconvolution is applied, so power agreement measures the amount of fine-scale variation rather than its correctness; the correlation statistic supplies that second test.

\subsubsection{Fixed weighted moments}

Our primary shape diagnostic uses a circular Gaussian weight of width $\sigma=10$ target pixels, truncated at radius 25 target pixels (approximately $0\farcs6$ and $1\farcs5$), fixed at the cutout centre.
For intensity $I_p$ and weight $w_p$ at position $\boldsymbol{r}_p$,
\begin{equation}
    F_w=\sum_p w_p I_p, \qquad
    \bar{\boldsymbol{r}}=\frac{\sum_p w_p I_p\boldsymbol{r}_p}{F_w},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    Q_{ij}=\frac{\sum_p w_p I_p(r_{p,i}-\bar r_i)(r_{p,j}-\bar r_j)}{F_w},
\end{equation}
and the ellipticity components are
\begin{equation}
    e_1=\frac{Q_{xx}-Q_{yy}}{Q_{xx}+Q_{yy}}, \qquad
    e_2=\frac{2Q_{xy}}{Q_{xx}+Q_{yy}}.
\end{equation}
Native NISP positions are expressed in target-pixel units so that the weight has the same angular size at both samplings.
A measurement succeeds when the aperture is fully covered, the weighted flux is positive, and the second-moment matrix is positive definite.
Negative pixels remain in the sums.

\subsubsection{Adaptive moments}

As an independent estimator we use \texttt{GalSim} \texttt{FindAdaptiveMom} \citep{galsim}, which iteratively fits an elliptical Gaussian weight, centroid, and size, without PSF correction.
The crop is 105 target pixels (21 native pixels) and the initial size is ten target pixels.
Fits with nonpositive size or amplitude, nonfinite outputs, or a centroid displacement exceeding ten target pixels are rejected.
Because the two estimators weight the light differently, each method is compared with a JWST reference measured by the same estimator.

\subsubsection{Paired errors and uncertainties}
\label{sc:paired}

Within each comparison, all methods are evaluated on the same galaxies, namely those with valid measurements in the reference and in every compared image.
We call this the common sample and report its size alongside each result.
For $N$ galaxies, the component error is
\begin{equation}
    {\rm RMSE}_e=\left[\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
    \left(\Delta e_{1,n}^2+\Delta e_{2,n}^2\right)\right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta e_i=e_i^{\rm prediction}-e_i^{\rm JWST}$, and the improvement relative to a baseline is $100[1-{\rm RMSE}_e({\rm model})/{\rm RMSE}_e({\rm baseline})]$ percent.

Uncertainty intervals resample the 18 $3\arcmin$ spatial blocks with replacement, keeping all galaxies and methods within a block together, so that both terms of each ratio use the same draws.
We use 2000 resamples unless stated otherwise and quote the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles.
These intervals are conditional on the COSMOS field, the selected models, and the common sample; they do not include variation across training seeds.

For the dependence on input signal-to-noise (S/N), we define a proxy as the Gaussian-weighted NISP flux divided by the quadrature sum of the weighted pixel RMS, with weight width two native pixels truncated at five.
The proxy neglects correlated noise.
Bins are $(-\infty,5)$, $[5,10)$, $[10,20)$, $[20,50)$, and $[50,\infty)$.

\subsubsection{Robustness to measurement choices}
\label{sc:robustness_method}

We test the sensitivity of the ResNet shape results to 21 measurement scenarios defined before inspecting results: changes to the weight width, aperture radius, and centre; background offsets of $\pm0.1$ outer-region RMS applied to each image; alternative adaptive-moment settings; and added input noise.
The scenarios are listed in Appendix~\ref{ap:robustness}.

\subsubsection{Controlled shear-response experiment}
\label{sc:shear_response_method}

We separately test sensitivity to a known applied shear using synthetic exponential galaxies rendered with \texttt{GalSim} and processed by the unchanged ResNet.
The grid contains half-light radii $0\farcs18$, $0\farcs35$, and $0\farcs65$, input S/N of 20, 50, and 100, and noiseless inputs.
Each galaxy has intrinsic distortion 0.35 at eight orientations; noisy cells use four noise realizations per orientation.
Native images are convolved with each of the four supplied NISP \YE\ stack effective PSFs, which include the pixel response.
A noiseless high-resolution control uses a Gaussian PSF of FWHM $0\farcs08$; it is an illustrative control, not a JWST simulation.

For each orientation and noise realization we render zero shear and $g_j=\pm0.01$ along each axis, reusing the same noise across shear branches and PSFs.
We measure adaptive distortions on native NISP, ResNet, and the control, keep the objects successful in all branches, and form
\begin{equation}
 R_{ij}=\frac{\langle e_i\rangle_{g_j=+0.01}-\langle e_i\rangle_{g_j=-0.01}}{0.02}.
 \label{eq:shear_response}
\end{equation}
We report $\mathrm{tr}(R)/\mathrm{tr}(R_{\rm intrinsic})$, where the denominator uses the analytically sheared intrinsic distortions, so that unity denotes the shear sensitivity of the intrinsic ensemble.
The four per-PSF ratios are averaged equally.
This pilot provides point estimates without uncertainty intervals and does not include the selection response needed for shear calibration.

\section{\label{sc:results}Results}

\begin{figure*}[t]

Figure~\ref{fig:pixel_comp} and Table~\ref{tab:regional} compare all methods on the same pixels.
The residual networks have the lowest source and outside-source RMSE, roughly 25\% below a single diffusion realization and 45\% below bilinear interpolation.
Averaging three diffusion realizations reduces its error but does not close the gap.

The spectral panels show a complementary trade-off.
At wavelengths of 6--15 target pixels, a single diffusion realization retains about 90\% of the observed source power but with a signed correlation of 0.60, whereas ResNet retains about 40\% of the power with a correlation of 0.74.
Diffusion thus produces more fine-scale structure, but less of it is in the right place; the residual networks produce smoother images that are better aligned with the reference.
Averaging diffusion realizations moves it towards the residual behaviour, with lower power and higher correlation.
Neither family recovers the full observed fine-scale power.

\subsection{ResNet reproducibility and model choice}
\label{sc:resnet_selection}

Three independent 50-epoch ResNet training runs give source RMSE values of 0.0601--0.0602\,MJy\,sr$^{-1}$ and outside-source values of 0.0139--0.0140, a between-seed scatter of 0.09\%.
The continuation experiments of Appendix~\ref{ap:continuations} reduce source RMSE by at most 2\%, with shape improvements that are either absent or marginal.
We therefore retain the original ResNet as the main model.
ResNet-D5 has slightly lower source RMSE and comparable shape performance, and we keep it as a comparator.

\subsection{Shape preservation}
\label{sc:shape_preservation}

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/shape_hexbin_v06.pdf}
    \caption{Observed ellipticity of native NISP, a single diffusion realization, ResNet, and ResNet-D5 (columns) against the same measurement on JWST F115W, for fixed weighted moments (top) and adaptive moments (bottom).
    Both components enter for each galaxy in the common sample (1446 fixed, 1498 adaptive, of 2118).
    Dashed: 1:1. Solid: Theil--Sen fit over $|e_{\mathrm{JWST}}|<0.5$; dotted: the native-NISP fit repeated.
    Each panel lists the slope and the RMSE about the 1:1 line.}
    \label{fig:ellipticity_comp}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:ellipticity_comp} compares the ellipticity of each image with the JWST ellipticity of the same galaxy.
For fixed weighted moments, the slope of the core relation rises from 0.68 (95\% interval 0.66--0.70) for native NISP to 0.80 (0.77--0.83) for both residual networks, and the RMSE about the 1:1 line falls from 0.201 to 0.162, a reduction of 19.3\% (16.6--21.9\%).
For adaptive moments the slopes are 0.61 (0.58--0.64) and 0.73 (0.71--0.75), with RMSE falling from 0.242 to 0.217, a reduction of 10.6\% (7.3--13.2\%).
Slope intervals use 500 resamples.
A single diffusion realization also steepens the slope, to 0.83 and 0.71, but widens the scatter, so its RMSE exceeds native NISP by 13.8\% and 22.4\%.
The residual-network gain comes from the steeper relation and from fewer large errors rather than from a narrower core.

Across the 21 measurement scenarios of Appendix~\ref{ap:robustness}, ResNet retains lower shape RMSE than native NISP in every case.
On the 1098 galaxies measurable in all scenarios, the reduction ranges from 8.2\% to 25.0\%.
Background offsets have the largest effect: subtracting 0.1 outer-region RMS from the reconstruction lowers both its success count and its RMSE reduction, from 19.0\% to 8.2\%.
The direction of improvement is stable; its size depends on the estimator and background treatment.

\subsection{Shape recovery versus input signal-to-noise}
\label{sc:shape_snr}

    \caption{Shape recovery versus input NISP S/N.
    Left and middle: reduction in ellipticity-component RMSE relative to native NISP for fixed weighted and adaptive moments; ochre circles, cobalt squares, and green triangles are a single diffusion realization, ResNet, and ResNet-D5. Whiskers are 95\% spatial-block intervals; lines guide the eye.

    \label{fig:shape_snr}

Figure~\ref{fig:shape_snr} shows that both residual networks reduce shape error relative to native NISP in every S/N bin and for both estimators.
For ResNet the reduction grows from 19.9\% (13.8--25.3\%) with fixed moments and 7.8\% (3.2--11.4\%) with adaptive moments at S/N $<5$ to 63.0\% (57.3--68.3\%) and 39.8\% (29.1--47.6\%) at S/N $\geq50$; ResNet-D5 behaves similarly.
A single diffusion realization is worse than native NISP below S/N 20 under both estimators and reaches 38.5\% (26.6--47.2\%) with fixed moments in the highest bin.

The low-S/N bins are a selected subset: only 29\% of fields enter the fixed-moment comparison and 47\% the adaptive one in the lowest bin, because the comparison requires a successful measurement in every image.
The trend with S/N is descriptive, since size, brightness, crowding, and redshift also vary between bins.

\subsection{Response to applied shear}
\label{sc:shear_response_results}

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/shear_response_v06.pdf}
    \caption{Normalized shear response $\mathrm{tr}(R)/\mathrm{tr}(R_{\rm intrinsic})$ of observed adaptive shapes for exponential galaxies with half-light radii $0\farcs18$ (a) and $0\farcs35$ (b), averaged over the four NISP effective PSFs.
    Charcoal circles: native NISP; cobalt squares: ResNet; orange diamonds: noiseless high-resolution control. The dotted line marks the intrinsic response.
    Points are estimates without uncertainty intervals.}
    \label{fig:shear_response}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:shear_response} shows a higher normalized shear response for ResNet than for native NISP in all six displayed size and S/N combinations.
At S/N 100 the ratios are 0.80 and 0.89 for ResNet at radii $0\farcs18$ and $0\farcs35$, against 0.42 and 0.64 for native NISP and 0.91 and 0.97 for the high-resolution control.
ResNet thus retains more of the applied distortion for compact galaxies, but remains below both the intrinsic and control responses.

The improvement is not uniform.
At radius $0\farcs65$ and S/N 20 the ResNet response (0.72) falls below native NISP (0.77); at S/N 50 and 100 ResNet is again higher (0.85 versus 0.81 and 0.92 versus 0.82).
Noiseless-input ResNet responses are 0.81, 0.92, and 0.95 at the three radii.
The two displayed radii were chosen in an earlier pilot for their larger gains and are not an unbiased size selection.
The experiment uses exponential profiles at fixed pixel phase, independent pixel noise, and no blending, and it conditions on successful measurements.

\section{\label{sc:discussion}Discussion}

The residual networks achieve lower image errors and stronger spatial correlation with the observed JWST reference, while diffusion retains more small-scale power (Fig.~\ref{fig:pixel_comp}).
Apparent sharpness is therefore an incomplete measure of reconstruction quality: a prediction can contain fine-scale variation without placing it where it appears in the reference.
The residual reconstructions are more accurate under every tested metric, although their reduced power shows that some observed detail is smoothed away.
Repeated training runs and the continuation experiments support the original ResNet as a reproducible and adequate choice; the small fixed-moment shape gain from PSF and noise conditioning (Appendix~\ref{ap:continuations}) merits further study with more varied PSFs.

The shape comparisons and controlled simulations show two useful consequences of reconstruction: observed ellipticities agree more closely with JWST, and compact simulated galaxies retain more sensitivity to an applied shear.
Both gains are conditional.
Low-S/N shape comparisons include only the subset of galaxies measurable in every image, and the largest simulated galaxies at low S/N show no response improvement.
As stated in Sect.~\ref{sc:methods}, these are comparisons between observed images in different bands, and the cross-band slopes are not multiplicative shear calibration factors.
Reaching the \Euclid\ requirement on multiplicative bias \citep{Cropper2013} would require a validated treatment of measurement and selection response across realistic morphologies, sizes, noise, blends, and spatially varying PSFs.

The scope of the sample also limits the conclusions.
Our galaxies lie in one COSMOS field and satisfy F814W $<25$ and $z_{\rm phot}<3$, so the results do not yet demonstrate performance on the optically fainter population that motivates near-infrared shape measurement.
The validation sample also informed model and measurement choices.
An independent evaluation on the 2876 reserved test galaxies, followed by tests across fields and populations, is the natural next step.
Future work should also quantify residual astrometric offsets and the effective PSFs of the input, target, and reconstructed images, which determine how image differences translate into morphological errors.

The improvements also motivate applications beyond cosmic shear, including morphology, deblending, and strong-lens searches.
Residual networks have been used to identify strong-lens candidates in the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys \citep{Huang_et_al_2021}, and learned reconstruction has been explored for deblending and sharpening \citep{superres_1, Reiman_Gohre_2019, Arcelin2021, Hemmati2022, superres_2}.
Each such task would need its own labelled sample and performance measure.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sc:conclusions}

Learned reconstruction improves the fidelity of NISP imaging to the observed JWST F115W reference.
Residual networks give lower image errors and stronger spatial correlation than the tested diffusion model, and the original ResNet reduces the observed ellipticity-component RMSE by approximately 19\% with fixed weighted moments and 11\% with adaptive moments.
Independent training runs reproduce its image errors, and controlled simulations show increased shear response for compact galaxies under the NISP effective PSFs.
These results support ResNet as a promising starting point for extracting more structural information from NISP imaging.
Evaluation on the reserved test sample and realistic simulations of instrumental, noise, and selection effects will establish how far these gains can support morphological and weak-lensing applications.

\section{ResNet continuation experiments}
\label{ap:continuations}

We tested whether short additional training improves the original ResNet.
All continuations start from the selected epoch-49 checkpoint with its optimizer state, run for ten epochs with identical batches and augmentations and a cosine learning-rate decay from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$, and select the checkpoint with the lowest validation source-region mean absolute error, with the unchanged parent as a candidate.

Two continuations vary the loss.
A control retains the original valid-pixel mean absolute error; the other gives equal weight to the source and outside-source errors defined in Sect.~\ref{sc:regional_structure}.
The control reduces source RMSE by 1.21\% and outside-source RMSE by 0.09\% relative to the parent.
The reweighted loss reduces source RMSE by 2.19\% but increases outside-source RMSE by 2.40\%, with no clear change in fixed or adaptive shape RMSE.

Two further continuations add PSF and noise information.
A small adapter after the first convolution receives the NISP RMS map, validity mask, and an $11\times11$ kernel representation of the associated stack PSF; its output is initialized to zero so that the initial prediction equals the parent.
One variant also receives a regularized inverse-filtered image with transfer function $H^*(1+\lambda)/(|H|^2+\lambda)$, $\lambda=0.05$.
Relative to the control, these reduce source RMSE by a further 0.12\% and 0.16\%, with 95\% paired intervals including zero.
The PSF and noise variant reduces fixed-moment shape RMSE by 0.98\% with an interval excluding zero, on 1627 common galaxies; neither variant clearly improves adaptive shapes on 1700 common galaxies.
The experiment is a warm start from one parent with four stack PSFs, so it does not characterize PSF-aware architectures in general.

\section{Measurement robustness scenarios}
\label{ap:robustness}

Starting from the nominal fixed moments, the 21 scenarios vary the Gaussian width from 10 to 8 or 12 target pixels, the aperture radius from 25 to 20 or 30 pixels, and the centre by $\pm2$ pixels along either axis.
Background tests shift the NISP, reconstruction, or JWST intensities by $\pm0.1$ times the outer-region RMS.
Adaptive-moment checks use the nominal settings, a 155-pixel crop, or an initial size of 15 pixels.
Three fixed-seed trials add Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.25 times the NISP RMS to the input and recompute the reconstruction and interpolations.
Each scenario compares ResNet with native NISP and the interpolations on its own common sample and on the 1098 galaxies measurable in all scenarios; intervals use 1000 spatial-block resamples.
Subtracting 0.1 outer-region RMS from the reconstruction reduces its successful measurements from 2045 to 1759 of 2118.
