\title{\Euclid\/: Deep-learning super-resolution of NISP imaging and its fidelity for galaxy shape measurement\thanks{This paper is published on behalf of the Euclid Consortium}}

\abstract{
\Euclid\ is imaging of order a billion galaxies over 14\,000\,deg$^2$ in one broad visible band and three near-infrared bands. Weak lensing shapes are currently measured only in the visible images. Near-infrared shapes could eventually enlarge the source sample and extend it to higher redshift, but the near-infrared pixels are three times coarser than the visible ones. As a step towards that goal, we investigate whether learned super-resolution of the near-infrared images improves image fidelity and observed shapes while preserving sensitivity to applied shear. We train a conditional diffusion model and two residual-network variants on \Euclid\ \YE-band cutouts paired with JWST/NIRCam F115W images of the same COSMOS field at five times finer sampling, and evaluate them on a separate validation sample against the observed F115W images. The generative diffusion model retains about 90\% of the observed small-scale power but places less of it correctly, whereas the deterministic residual networks produce smoother images with 25\% lower reconstruction error and stronger spatial correlation with JWST; these reconstruction errors are consistent across three training seeds. Relative to the native \Euclid\ images, the residual network reduces the root-mean-square ellipticity error against JWST by approximately 19\% with fixed weighted moments and 11\% with adaptive moments. The gains grow with signal-to-noise ratio, reaching 63\% with fixed moments and 40\% with adaptive moments for the brightest galaxies, whereas at low signal-to-noise ratio only a subset of galaxies yields measurable shapes. Controlled simulations using the \Euclid\ near-infrared point-spread functions show increased shear response for compact galaxies after reconstruction, but the gain depends on size and noise, and responses remain below the intrinsic value. At 0.5\,ms per galaxy, the residual network can process the full first \Euclid\ data release in under a day. These results support residual networks as a practical route to improving observed-shape fidelity in \Euclid\ near-infrared imaging. Establishing calibrated shear accuracy will require realistic simulations and independent validation that account for noise, instrumental response, cross-band differences, and measurement selection.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sc:intro}

Weak gravitational lensing is one of the primary cosmological probes of Stage IV wide-field surveys \citep{Laureijs2011, Chang2013}, and its statistical precision places demanding requirements on the control of systematic errors. Shape measurement in particular has required a sustained, community-wide effort: successive blind challenges \citep{Heymans2006, Massey2007, Mandelbaum2015} and calibration frameworks based on image simulations and self-calibration \citep[e.g.,][]{Huff2017, Sheldon2017, EC_Congedo2024} have been devoted
to measuring and removing biases in shape estimators. For \Euclid, multiplicative shear biases must be controlled to $10^{-3}$ \citep{Cropper2013}. This requirement is sensitive to the details of the galaxy population: replacing the morphologies in the Euclid Flagship simulation with distributions learned from \Euclid\ imaging shifts the multiplicative bias at the per cent level
\citep{EC_ShapeDist2026}, several times the allowed budget. Any new shape-measurement technique must therefore be tested for bias and calibrated on realistic simulations before its outputs can support cosmological inference.

Deep learning has meanwhile become integral to astronomical data analysis, with applications ranging from image generation and outlier detection to photometric redshifts and deblending \citep{Arcelin2021, Hemmati2022, Sampson2024, Scognamiglio2025}. Super-resolution, the task of inferring high-resolution spatial
information from lower-resolution inputs \citep{Anwar2019}, has been applied from radio interferometry to solar and ground-based optical imaging \citep[e.g.,][]{Dabbech_2022, Song2024, Sukurdeep2025}. A closely related approach learns the mapping between two instruments that observe the same sky at different resolutions: \citet{Hemmati2022} trained a conditional generative adversarial network on paired
Subaru/HSC and HST images to deblend ground-based galaxy images, and \citet{Kahn2026} applied the same type of network to the same HSC-to-HST translation and reported improvements by factors of 2 to 10 in the accuracy of recovered morphological parameters. Space-based imaging of the same fields thus provides both the training target and an independent reference for judging what the reconstruction has recovered.

\Euclid\ offers a natural setting for this approach. Its weak lensing shapes are measured in the visible band with VIS \citep[$0\farcs1$ pixels;][]{EuclidSkyVIS}, while the near-infrared imaging from NISP \citep[$0\farcs3$ pixels;][]{EuclidSkyNISP} is three times coarser in sampling and serves photometry and photometric redshifts rather than shape measurement. Galaxies that are well detected in the near-infrared but lack usable VIS shapes, including red and high-redshift sources, are therefore excluded from the weak lensing sample. Studies with HST/CANDELS imaging have demonstrated the potential of near-infrared shapes: \citet{Lee2018} compared ellipticities in optical and near-infrared images degraded to survey quality, and \citet{Finner2023} examined WFC3-IR PSF modelling and weak lensing systematics. Super-resolved NISP images could extend the range of galaxies with useful shapes, increasing the source density and redshift coverage available to lensing analyses. The same gain would serve galaxy science: at $z\gtrsim1$ the NISP bands sample rest-frame optical light and so trace the stellar mass distribution rather than individual star-forming regions, yet \Euclid\ morphological measurements, from S\'ersic fits to machine-learning classifications, are currently made on VIS imaging \citep[e.g.,][]{EC_Q1_Quilley2025, EC_Q1_Walmsley2025}. Sharper near-infrared images over the Euclid Wide Survey would support measurements of structure, merger signatures, and blending for a far larger sample than
space-based near-infrared imaging has covered to date.

This paper is a step in that direction, with weak lensing as the primary test case because it sets the most stringent requirements on measured shapes. We train deep-learning super-resolution models on \Euclid\ NISP images of the COSMOS field and evaluate them against overlapping JWST/NIRCam imaging, which provides a factor of five finer sampling and an independent reference for every galaxy (Sect.~\ref{sc:observations}). We compare a conditional diffusion model \citep{sohldickstein2015, Ho2020}, which generates images through successive denoising steps, with a residual network built from residual dense blocks \citep{Wang2018}, which predicts an image in a single pass (Sect.~\ref{sc:methods}). We evaluate both on image fidelity, spatial structure, and observed ellipticity, examine how shape errors depend on input signal-to-noise, and test the response of the residual network to an applied shear in controlled simulations (Sects.~\ref{sc:measurement} and \ref{sc:results}). Section~\ref{sc:discussion} summarises the results and the steps needed to extend them to the fainter near-infrared population and to
calibrated shear measurement.

\section{\label{sc:observations}Observations}

We use \Euclid\ NISP \YE-band imaging \citep{EuclidSkyNISP} and
overlapping JWST/NIRCam F115W imaging \citep{jwst_nircam} in the
COSMOS field \citep{Scoville_2007}. The NISP images are four co-added
stacks processed with the first-data-release (DR1) version of the
NIR pipeline \citep{DR1-TP003} onto a grid of $0\farcs2967$ per
pixel. The effective point-spread function (PSF) supplied with each stack has a FWHM of
approximately $0\farcs46$, and the Wide Survey reaches a $5\,\sigma$
point-source depth of approximately 24.5\,AB in \YE\
\citep{EuclidSkyOverview}. The JWST images are COSMOS-Web F115W
mosaics \citep[version 0.8, preceding the public data release
of][]{Franco2025} at $0\farcs06$ per pixel, with a measured PSF
FWHM of approximately $0\farcs056$ in F115W \citep{Zhuang2024} and a
$5\,\sigma$ point-source depth of 26.7--27.4\,AB depending on exposure
coverage \citep{Franco2025}. We
use the $0\farcs06$ rather than the $0\farcs03$ mosaics because the
NISP PSF, not the target sampling, limits the recoverable detail, and
the coarser grid gives an integer $5\times$ ratio to the NISP pixel at
a quarter of the output size. The overlap between the NISP stacks and
the COSMOS-Web footprint covers approximately 0.5\,deg$^2$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sample_overview}a). The \YE\ band (950--1212\,nm;
\citealt{Schirmer-EP18}) and F115W (1013--1282\,nm) overlap but are not
identical, so differences in stellar populations and dust contribute
to differences between the images.

We select COSMOS2020 Classic galaxy-class objects
\citep[\texttt{lp\_type}$=0$;][]{cosmos2020_classic} with ACS F814W
AB magnitude $<25$, EAZY photometric redshift $0.01<\zph<3$,
and $4<\texttt{FLUX\_RADIUS}<500$ catalogue pixels, where the radius
encloses half of the flux in the PSF-homogenised $K_s$ image. The
magnitude threshold follows the approximate $riz<25$ \Euclid-depth
selection of \citet[Sect.~5.2]{shooby_calibration}, with F814W as an
optical-band proxy. These cuts define a sample for
resolved-morphology analysis; they do not implement a survey-specific
shape-quality selection.

The inputs comprise four NISP \YE-band stacks and 19 F115W mosaics,
of which all four stacks and 18 mosaics contribute accepted galaxies.
Of the 127\,162 COSMOS2020 galaxy-class objects within the overlap
region, 39\,024 satisfy the selection above; the extraction retains
22\,810 paired galaxies after coverage, pixel-quality, and
spatial-buffer cuts (Sect.~\ref{sc:preprocessing}). We assign
disjoint $3\arcmin$ sky blocks to training, validation, and test
subsets, excluding galaxies within $10\arcsec$ of block boundaries,
which gives 17\,816 training, 2118 validation, and 2876 test
galaxies. Training data fit the model weights and normalisation
scales; validation data support checkpoint selection and development
of the models and measurement protocol. All comparisons of observed
galaxies in this paper use the validation sample. The test sample is
reserved for an independent evaluation of the final procedure.

The paired sample is small compared with the imaging to which the
trained models can be applied, because only the NISP image is needed
at inference; the JWST reference enters only through training and
evaluation. DR1 covers approximately 1900\,deg$^2$ of the Wide Survey,
corresponding to of order $10^{8}$ galaxies, processed with the same
pipeline version as the COSMOS stacks used here, so the trained
networks should transfer to the full release with at most a brief
fine-tuning on the released COSMOS products. Later releases will
extend the same procedure to the full 14\,000\,deg$^2$ survey.

Figure~\ref{fig:sample_overview} shows the block assignment across
the field and an example NISP--JWST pair.
Figure~\ref{fig:sample_properties} compares the magnitude and
redshift distributions of the three subsets with the parent
catalogue: the three subsets are consistent with one another and
follow the parent distribution in magnitude, while the
$\zph<3$ cut removes the high-redshift tail of the parent
sample.

For each galaxy we extract a $41\times41$ NISP cutout and construct a
$205\times205$ target image over the same approximately $12\arcsec$
field, sampling the sky five times more finely than the NISP input.
This change in sampling does not by itself define the recovered
resolution: the targets retain the JWST PSF, mosaic resampling, and
the interpolation described in Sect.~\ref{sc:preprocessing}. We
therefore evaluate reconstructions against the observed F115W images
rather than against a deconvolved truth.

\section{\label{sc:methods}Super-resolution models}

We compare a conditional diffusion model and two variants of a
residual convolutional network for reconstructing JWST F115W images
from NISP \YE-band observations. The two families differ in what
they are trained to produce. The diffusion model is generative: it
learns the distribution of JWST images consistent with a given NISP
input and draws a sample from it through repeated updates starting
from random noise, so different random starts give different
plausible reconstructions. The residual networks are deterministic:
each predicts a single image in one pass, and training with an
absolute-error loss drives that prediction towards a central estimate
of the possible JWST images, which tends to smooth away detail that
the NISP input does not constrain. A generative model can therefore
produce sharper images and a deterministic one more conservative
ones, and which is preferable depends on whether the task rewards
realistic fine structure or accuracy pixel by pixel; we return to
this in Sect.~\ref{sc:results}. All models learn from the paired
training images, so their outputs depend on both the NISP
measurements and the galaxy population in the training sample. This
section describes the data preparation, the two architectures, and
their training; Sect.~\ref{sc:measurement} describes how the
reconstructions are evaluated.

\subsection{Image preparation}
\label{sc:preprocessing}

We construct paired cutouts using the COSMOS2020 coordinates and the
world coordinate system (WCS) of each mosaic. The NISP cutout
contains $41\times41$ native pixels, extracted with \texttt{Astropy}
\citep{astropy_latest} without rotation or interpolation. We define a
$205\times205$ output grid with the same footprint and orientation,
subdividing each NISP pixel into $5\times5$ target pixels of
approximately $0\farcs06$. Each target pixel is mapped through both
WCS solutions to the JWST mosaic and its surface brightness is
obtained by bilinear interpolation \citep{scipy}. We apply no
additional shifts fitted from the galaxy images, so residual
astrometric offsets between the two surveys can contribute to
reconstruction differences.

A NISP pixel is valid when its science value is finite, its
data-quality flag is zero, and its supplied root-mean-square (RMS)
uncertainty is finite and positive. The version 0.8 JWST mosaics
carry no weight or error extensions, so a JWST pixel is valid when
its science value is finite and nonzero. An interpolated target pixel
is valid only when all contributing source pixels are valid. We
require at least 95\% valid pixels in each cutout and complete
coverage of the central $5\times5$ NISP pixels.

We estimate and subtract a constant background per image from the
sigma-clipped median of the pixels outside a central square of
$21\times21$ NISP pixels (about $6\farcs2$). NISP fluxes are
converted to MJy\,sr$^{-1}$ using the local pixel solid angle; JWST
images are already in these units. Each band is then divided by a
fixed scale determined from the training set alone: the median NISP
pixel RMS (0.02307\,MJy\,sr$^{-1}$) and the median JWST outer-region
standard deviation (0.01365\,MJy\,sr$^{-1}$). Individual galaxies
are not normalised by their own flux, so the networks see surface
brightness and noise on a common absolute scale. Invalid pixels are
set to zero after scaling, and negative intensities are retained.
All image and shape comparisons in Sects.~\ref{sc:measurement} and
\ref{sc:results} are made on background-subtracted intensities in
MJy\,sr$^{-1}$, after inverting this scaling (and, for the diffusion
model, the transformation of Sect.~\ref{sc:diffusion_model}) and
without clipping.

\subsection{Diffusion model}
\label{sc:diffusion_model}

The diffusion model follows the image-refinement approach of
\citet{SR3}, based on denoising diffusion models
\citep{Ho2020, Nichol2021}. A diffusion model is a neural network
trained to remove noise from an image; generation reverses a gradual
noising process by applying the network repeatedly, each step
removing a little noise, so that an image consistent with the
conditioning input emerges from a random field. During training, we
add a known amount of Gaussian noise to a JWST cutout and train the network to recover the
original image. Repeating this at many noise levels teaches the model
to proceed from a random field towards an F115W reconstruction
(Fig.~\ref{fig:diff_model}a).

The NISP image is placed on the output grid by bilinear interpolation
as a spatially aligned conditioning input, with a second map
identifying valid NISP pixels so that the network can distinguish
missing data from faint emission. Both bands are passed through
$\mathrm{asinh}(I/3)$, where $I$ is the scaled, background-subtracted
intensity of Sect.~\ref{sc:preprocessing}. Because the diffusion
process adds noise of unit variance, the target must have a dynamic
range of order unity; this transformation is linear below about three
times the pixel noise and logarithmic above it, so it compresses
bright galaxy cores while retaining negative values. It is inverted
before evaluation.

The network is a U-Net, a convolutional encoder--decoder that halves
the image resolution in successive stages and then restores it; the
encoder levels contain 32, 64, and 128 feature maps, with a central
stage of 128 and a symmetric decoder
(Fig.~\ref{fig:diff_model}b). Skip connections pass encoder features
to the corresponding decoder level, and each stage contains two
residual blocks. The noisy image is the only input channel; the NISP
image, its validity map, and the current noise level modulate the
features of every stage through learned rescalings and offsets (FiLM;
\citealt{Perez2017}). Images are padded from 205 to 208 pixels
internally, so that the three halvings of resolution in the encoder
are exact, and cropped on output.

For each training pair we sample one of 1000 noise levels from a cosine schedule
\citep{Nichol2021}, which spaces the levels so that the signal is
removed gradually rather than mostly in the first steps. Writing the transformed target as $x_0$ and a standard Gaussian field as $\epsilon$, the noisy image at step $t$ and the quantity the network is trained to predict
\citep[the velocity parameterisation of][]{Salimans2022} are
\begin{equation}
    x_t=a_t x_0+b_t\epsilon, \qquad v_t=a_t\epsilon-b_t x_0,
\end{equation}
where $a_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$, $b_t=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$, and $\bar\alpha_t$ is the fraction of signal variance retained at step $t$. From the prediction $\hat v_t$, the reconstructed target is $\hat x_0=a_t x_t-b_t\hat v_t$. We minimise
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\rm diff}
    =\left\langle(\hat v_t-v_t)^2\right\rangle_V
    +0.1\left\langle|\hat x_0-x_0|\right\rangle_V,
\end{equation}
where $\langle\cdot\rangle_V$ averages over valid JWST pixels within each galaxy and then over the batch, in the transformed coordinates.

To generate an image (Fig.~\ref{fig:diff_model}c), we start from a Gaussian field and apply 50 denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM) updates \citep{Song2021DDIM} conditioned on the NISP image and mask. No further noise is added during generation, so a fixed initial field gives a reproducible reconstruction, while a different initial field gives a different one.

\subsection{Residual networks}
\label{sc:residual_model}

The residual network produces an F115W estimate in a single pass. It first extracts features on the $41\times41$ grid, then enlarges them to the $205\times205$ output (Fig.~\ref{fig:resnet_model}). We compare two variants with the same feature extraction and different enlargement paths, a two-stage network (ResNet) and a direct-$5\times$ network (ResNet-D5), each trained separately.

Feature extraction (Fig.~\ref{fig:resnet_model}a) combines residual learning \citep{ResNet} with the dense reuse of intermediate features used in image super-resolution \citep{Wang2018}. In residual learning, each block predicts a correction that is added to its own input rather than replacing it, which keeps deep networks trainable and lets the layers concentrate on the difference between input and target; in a dense block, each convolution also receives the outputs of all earlier convolutions in the block. An initial convolution produces 64 feature maps, which pass through eight residual dense blocks (Fig.~\ref{fig:resnet_model}c). Within each block, four $3\times3$ convolutions with leaky ReLU activation (slope 0.2) each add 32 feature maps to the accumulated input, a fifth combines them into 64 maps, and the result is added to the block input without rescaling. After the eight blocks, a convolution combines the features and a long skip adds back the initial maps. A further module applies a fixed $3\times3$ Laplacian filter to each of the 64 feature maps, adds the response to the features, and processes the result with learned convolutions, which emphasises the sharp transitions that super-resolution must recover; channel weights computed from each map's mean response \citep{hu2019} then rescale
the maps before enlargement.

Both enlargement paths (Fig.~\ref{fig:resnet_model}b) use pixel shuffling, in which a convolution predicts extra feature maps that are rearranged onto a finer grid \citep{Shi2016PixelShuffle}. ResNet doubles the grid twice, from 41 to 82 to 164 pixels, predicts an image, and resizes it bilinearly to $205\times205$ inside the network, so that the loss is evaluated on the target grid. This final non-integer resampling slightly smooths the output, which motivated ResNet-D5: it enlarges in one step from 41 to 205 pixels and predicts directly on the target grid. Because a $5\times$ shuffle requires 25 rather than four feature maps per output map, ResNet-D5 has about 0.6 million more parameters (Table~\ref{tab:training_settings}), so the comparison includes a change in capacity as well as in resampling.

Both variants take the linearly scaled NISP image as their only input; invalid pixels are zero after scaling, and adding the validity mask as a second input is among the variations tested in
Sect.~\ref{sc:training}. Each network is trained with the mean absolute difference between prediction and JWST target over valid pixels, an objective that is less sensitive to the brightest pixels than a squared error. It acts on linear intensity, whereas the diffusion loss acts in asinh coordinates.

\subsection{Training and model selection}
\label{sc:training}

All three models use the same 17\,816 training galaxies and a budget of 50 epochs at batch size eight (111\,350 updates). We apply random $90\degr$ rotations and reflections consistently to each paired batch; validation images are not augmented. Training uses the Adam optimiser with a one-cycle learning-rate
schedule, in which the rate rises to a peak of $10^{-4}$ and then
decays to near zero by the end of training. All losses exclude invalid target pixels and average over the valid pixels of each galaxy before averaging over the batch, so that each galaxy has equal weight. Table~\ref{tab:training_settings} summarises the
settings. The equal update budget does not imply equal computation, since the models differ in parameter count, objective, and generation cost. On a single NVIDIA L40S GPU per model, training took 1.2\,h for ResNet, 1.4\,h for ResNet-D5, and 3.8\,h for the diffusion model, and producing one reconstruction takes 0.5\,ms for ResNet and 125\,ms for a 50-step diffusion draw.

We select the checkpoint with the lowest validation mean absolute image error. For the residual networks this uses all 2118 validation galaxies. For diffusion, where generation requires 50 network evaluations per galaxy, we monitor a fixed random subset of 256
validation galaxies with fixed initial noise fields; the selected checkpoint is then evaluated on the full validation sample like the other models. Shape measurements are not used for checkpoint selection.

Three further experiments test how much the results depend on choices that are not fixed by the data. First, to assess the variation of the diffusion output with its initial noise field, we generate three independently seeded images per validation galaxy and also evaluate their mean in linear surface brightness as a separate reconstruction. Second, to assess the variation between training runs, we repeat the full ResNet training with two additional seeds,
keeping everything else fixed; the original checkpoint was designated the main model before these runs. Third, we tested four ten-epoch continuations of the selected ResNet: a control that continues with the original loss; a loss that gives equal weight to errors inside and outside the source region defined in Sect.~\ref{sc:regional}; and two variants that receive the NISP RMS map, validity mask, and stack effective PSF as additional inputs through a three-convolution side branch on the first layer whose final convolution is initialised to zero, so that each continuation starts by reproducing the parent model exactly. One of these two variants additionally receives the difference between a Tikhonov-regularised deconvolution of the NISP image by the stack PSF (regularisation 0.05, normalised to conserve flux) and the NISP image itself. The outcome of these experiments is reported in Sect.~\ref{sc:resnet_selection}.

\section{\label{sc:measurement}Image and shape measurements}

We compare each reconstruction with the JWST reference, the native
NISP image, and bilinear and bicubic enlargements of NISP on the
target grid; Fig.~\ref{fig:galaxy_examples} shows these images for
five validation fields. Interpolation uses background-subtracted
linear intensities.

Throughout, the reference is the \emph{observed} F115W image. It
retains the JWST PSF, instrumental noise, resampling, and the
\YE--F115W band difference, and we apply no PSF deconvolution or
band correction. Agreement with this reference therefore measures
fidelity to the observed JWST image, not intrinsic galaxy shape or
calibrated gravitational shear. We state this once here; the shape
statistics in Sect.~\ref{sc:results} should all be read in this
sense.

\subsection{Regional errors and spatial structure}
\label{sc:regional_structure}

We measure reconstruction errors in the central $105\times105$
target pixels (approximately $6\farcs3$) of all 2118 validation
objects, separately within and outside source regions. Source masks
are defined from the JWST reference and shared by all methods: we
smooth the reference with a one-pixel Gaussian, detect connected
regions of at least ten pixels that exceed the median by twice the
sigma-clipped standard deviation of the smoothed reference in an
annulus 70--95 target pixels from the centre, fill holes, and dilate
by three pixels. Errors are the per-object RMSE in signed,
background-subtracted intensity, averaged over galaxies with equal
weight.

Pixel errors alone do not distinguish a reconstruction that lacks
fine structure from one that places fine structure in the wrong
place. To separate the amount of spatial variation from its
agreement with the reference, we compute two-dimensional Fourier
transforms of each region after mean removal and tapering with a
separable Tukey window ($\alpha=0.5$) multiplied by a three-pixel
inward taper at the region boundary, identical for every model and
the reference, and average within annuli of spatial frequency $k$.
We report the ratio of model to reference power and the signed
correlation
\begin{equation}
 r(k)=\frac{\mathrm{Re}\langle F_{\rm model} F_{\rm JWST}^{*}\rangle_k}
 {\sqrt{\langle |F_{\rm model}|^2\rangle_k\langle |F_{\rm JWST}|^2\rangle_k}},
\end{equation}
where $\langle\cdot\rangle_k$ averages over the annulus. Because the
transform requires every pixel, these statistics use the 1363
cutouts with no invalid pixels; we report the geometric mean of
per-object power ratios and the arithmetic mean of per-object
correlations. Summary values are quoted at wavelengths of 6--15
target pixels ($0\farcs36$--$0\farcs9$), between the JWST PSF scale
and the NISP Nyquist wavelength of $0\farcs59$. The reference
spectra include noise, and no noise subtraction, PSF correction, or
window deconvolution is applied, so a power ratio near unity means
that the reconstruction contains as much fine-scale variation as the
reference, not that the variation is correct; the correlation
supplies that second test.

\subsection{Fixed weighted moments}
\label{sc:fixed_moments}

Our primary shape diagnostic uses a circular Gaussian weight of
width $\sigma=10$ target pixels, truncated at radius 25 target
pixels (approximately $0\farcs6$ and $1\farcs5$), fixed at the cutout
centre, that is, at the catalogue position. For intensity $I_p$ and
weight $w_p$ at position $\boldsymbol{r}_p$,
\begin{equation}
    F_w=\sum_p w_p I_p, \qquad
    \bar{\boldsymbol{r}}=\frac{\sum_p w_p I_p\boldsymbol{r}_p}{F_w},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    Q_{ij}=\frac{\sum_p w_p I_p(r_{p,i}-\bar r_i)(r_{p,j}-\bar r_j)}{F_w},
\end{equation}
and the ellipticity components are
\begin{equation}
    e_1=\frac{Q_{xx}-Q_{yy}}{Q_{xx}+Q_{yy}}, \qquad
    e_2=\frac{2Q_{xy}}{Q_{xx}+Q_{yy}}.
    \label{eq:ellipticity}
\end{equation}
Native NISP positions are expressed in target-pixel units so that
the weight has the same angular size at both samplings. A
measurement succeeds when the aperture is fully covered, the weighted
flux is positive, and the second-moment matrix is positive definite.
Negative pixels remain in the sums.

\subsection{Adaptive moments}
\label{sc:adaptive_moments}

As an independent estimator we use \texttt{GalSim}
\texttt{FindAdaptiveMom} \citep{galsim}, which iteratively fits an
elliptical Gaussian weight, centroid, and size, without PSF
correction. The crop is a square of 105 target pixels (21 native
pixels) and the initial size is ten target pixels. Fits with
nonpositive size or amplitude, nonfinite outputs, or a centroid
displacement exceeding ten target pixels are rejected. Because the
two estimators weight the light differently, each method is compared
with a JWST reference measured by the same estimator.

\subsection{Paired errors and uncertainties}
\label{sc:paired}

Within each comparison, all methods are evaluated on the same
galaxies, namely those with valid measurements in the reference and
in every compared image. We call this the common sample and report
its size alongside each result. For $N$ galaxies, the component
error is
\begin{equation}
    {\rm RMSE}_e=\left[\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
    \left(\Delta e_{1,n}^2+\Delta e_{2,n}^2\right)\right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta e_i=e_i^{\rm prediction}-e_i^{\rm JWST}$, and the
improvement relative to a baseline is
$100[1-{\rm RMSE}_e({\rm model})/{\rm RMSE}_e({\rm baseline})]$
per cent. We also fit the slope of measured against reference
ellipticity, with both components pooled, using the Theil--Sen
estimator over $|e^{\rm JWST}|<0.5$; a slope below unity indicates
that the image underestimates the reference ellipticity, as expected
when the coarser PSF and sampling round off the light distribution.

Uncertainty intervals resample the 18 $3\arcmin$ spatial blocks with
replacement, keeping all galaxies and methods within a block
together, so that both terms of each ratio use the same draws. We
use 2000 resamples unless stated otherwise (500 for the slopes) and
quote the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles, referred to below as 95\%
intervals. These intervals are conditional on the COSMOS field, the
selected models, and the common sample; they do not include
variation across training seeds.

For the dependence on input signal-to-noise (S/N), we define a proxy
as the Gaussian-weighted NISP flux divided by the quadrature sum of
the weighted pixel RMS values, with weight width two native pixels
truncated at five. The proxy neglects correlated noise. Bins are
$(-\infty,5)$, $[5,10)$, $[10,20)$, $[20,50)$, and $[50,\infty)$.

\subsection{Robustness to measurement choices}
\label{sc:robustness_method}

We test the sensitivity of the ResNet shape results to 21
measurement scenarios defined before inspecting results. Starting
from the nominal fixed moments, these vary the Gaussian width to 8
or 12 target pixels, the aperture radius to 20 or 30 pixels, and the
centre by $\pm2$ pixels along either axis; shift the NISP,
reconstruction, or JWST intensities by $\pm0.1$ outer-region RMS;
use a 155-pixel adaptive-moment crop or an initial size of 15
pixels; and add Gaussian noise of 0.25 times the NISP RMS to the
input in three fixed-seed trials before reconstructing. Each scenario
compares ResNet with native NISP and the interpolations on its own
common sample and on the galaxies measurable in all scenarios, with
1000 spatial-block resamples.

\subsection{Controlled shear-response experiment}
\label{sc:shear_response_method}

The comparisons above measure agreement with observed shapes. They
do not show whether a small change in shape, such as that produced
by gravitational shear, survives reconstruction. We therefore test
sensitivity to a known applied shear using synthetic exponential
galaxies rendered with \texttt{GalSim} and processed by the ResNet
trained on the real data, without any retraining on simulations. The
grid contains half-light radii $0\farcs18$, $0\farcs35$, and
$0\farcs65$, input S/N of 20, 50, and 100, and noiseless inputs at
the S/N $=50$ flux. Input S/N uses the same Gaussian-weighted proxy
as for the observed galaxies (Sect.~\ref{sc:paired}), with uniform
pixel noise of 0.028\,MJy\,sr$^{-1}$, evaluated on the noiseless,
unsheared profile after PSF convolution to set the flux, which is
then held fixed across shears and orientations. Each galaxy has
intrinsic distortion 0.35, in the convention of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:ellipticity}), at eight orientations; noisy cells use
four noise realisations per orientation. Native images are convolved
with each of the four supplied NISP \YE\ stack effective PSFs, which
include the pixel response, and sampled at the NISP pixel scale. A
noiseless high-resolution control uses a Gaussian PSF of FWHM
$0\farcs08$ on the target grid; it is an illustrative control, not a
JWST simulation.

For each orientation and noise realisation we render zero shear and
$g_j=\pm0.01$ along each axis, reusing the same noise across shear
branches and PSFs. We measure adaptive distortions on native NISP,
ResNet, and the control, keep the objects successful in all
branches, and form
\begin{equation}
 R_{ij}=\frac{\langle e_i\rangle_{g_j=+0.01}-\langle e_i\rangle_{g_j=-0.01}}{0.02}.
 \label{eq:shear_response}
\end{equation}
We report $\mathrm{tr}(R)/\mathrm{tr}(R_{\rm intrinsic})$, where the
denominator is the same quantity computed from the intrinsic
distortions sheared analytically, without PSF, pixelisation, or
noise, so that unity denotes the shear sensitivity of the intrinsic
ensemble and smaller values denote shear signal lost to the PSF,
sampling, noise, or reconstruction. The four per-PSF ratios are
averaged equally. This pilot provides point estimates without
uncertainty intervals and does not include the selection response
needed for shear calibration.

The residual networks recover the source separation and overall
morphology of the reference; diffusion produces sharper but more
variable structure. The population statistics below quantify this
impression.

distribution are averaged towards its centre. Neither family
recovers the full observed fine-scale power.

\subsection{Shape preservation}
\label{sc:shape_preservation}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/shape_hexbin_v07.pdf}

    Solid: Theil--Sen fit over $|e_{\mathrm{JWST}}|<0.5$. Each panel

    \label{fig:ellipticity_comp}

Of the 2118 validation galaxies, a fixed-moment measurement succeeds
on 1662 in the JWST reference, 1741 in native NISP, and 1786 in
the ResNet reconstruction, and the adaptive-moment counts are 1767,
1685, and 1742. The common samples of 1446 and 1498 galaxies are
therefore set mainly by the reference for fixed moments and by native
NISP for adaptive moments, and reconstruction increases the number
of galaxies with a measurable shape by 45 and 57 relative to native
NISP.

Figure~\ref{fig:ellipticity_comp} compares the ellipticity of each
image with the JWST ellipticity of the same galaxy. For fixed
weighted moments, the slope over $|e_{\mathrm{JWST}}|<0.5$ rises
from 0.68 (95\% interval 0.66--0.70) for native NISP to 0.80
(0.77--0.83) for both residual networks, and the RMSE about the 1:1
line falls from 0.201 to 0.162, a reduction of 19.3\%
(16.6--21.9\%). For adaptive moments the slopes are 0.61
(0.58--0.64) and 0.73 (0.71--0.75), with RMSE falling from 0.242 to
0.217, a reduction of 10.6\% (7.3--13.2\%). The intercepts of the
fitted relations are below 0.01 for native NISP and the residual
networks, the $e_1$ intercept of about $+0.01$ is already present in
native NISP, and $e_1$ and $e_2$ give the same slopes within their
intervals, so the residual networks introduce no measurable additive
offset or component-dependent response. A single diffusion
realisation also steepens the slope, to 0.83 and 0.71, but widens the
scatter, so its RMSE exceeds native NISP by 13.8\% and 22.4\%, and its
component intercepts reach $0.02$--$0.04$. Averaging three
realisations removes most of this penalty, to an RMSE 6.8\%
(3.5--10.0\%) below native NISP with fixed moments and 2.4\%
($-0.7$ to 5.9\%) above it with adaptive moments, still well short of
the residual networks. The residual-network gain comes from the
steeper relation and from a narrower error distribution throughout:
with fixed moments the median and 68th percentile of $|\Delta e|$
fall from 0.086 and 0.144 to 0.060 and 0.106, fractional reductions
of 30\% and 26\% that exceed the RMSE reduction, so the remaining
RMSE is set increasingly by a tail of large errors.

Across the 21 measurement scenarios of
Sect.~\ref{sc:robustness_method}, ResNet retains lower shape RMSE
than native NISP in every case. On the 1098 galaxies measurable in
all scenarios, the nominal reduction is 19.0\% (RMSE 0.154 to 0.125)
and the scenarios span 8.2\% to 25.0\%. Background offsets have the
largest effect: subtracting 0.1 outer-region RMS from the
reconstruction lowers its successful measurements from 1786 to 1544
of 2118 and, on each scenario's own common sample, the RMSE
reduction from 19.6\% to 10.7\%. The direction of improvement is
stable; its size depends on the estimator, the background treatment,
and the galaxies retained.

\subsection{Shape recovery versus input signal-to-noise}
\label{sc:shape_snr}

Figure~\ref{fig:shape_snr} shows that both residual networks reduce
shape error relative to native NISP in every S/N bin and for both
estimators. For ResNet the reduction grows from 19.9\%
(13.8--25.3\%) with fixed moments and 7.8\% (3.2--11.4\%) with
adaptive moments at S/N $<5$ to 63.0\% (57.3--68.3\%) and 39.8\%
(29.1--47.6\%) at S/N $\geq50$; ResNet-D5 behaves similarly. A
single diffusion realisation is worse than native NISP below S/N 20
under both estimators and reaches 38.5\% (26.6--47.2\%) with fixed
moments in the highest bin: where the input constrains the shape
weakly, the sampled fine structure adds scatter rather than
information.

The low-S/N bins are a selected subset: only 29\% of fields enter
the fixed-moment comparison and 47\% the adaptive one in the lowest
bin, because the comparison requires a successful measurement in
every image. The trend with S/N is descriptive, since size,
brightness, crowding, and redshift also vary between bins.

\subsection{ResNet reproducibility and model choice}
\label{sc:resnet_selection}

Three independent 50-epoch ResNet training runs give source RMSE
values of 0.0601--0.0602\,MJy\,sr$^{-1}$ and outside-source values
of 0.0139--0.0140, a relative between-seed scatter of 0.09\%, far
below the differences between methods in
Table~\ref{tab:regional}. The continuations of
Sect.~\ref{sc:training} give small gains: the original-loss control
reduces source RMSE by 1.2\% relative to the parent, the reweighted
loss by 2.2\% at the cost of a 2.4\% increase outside sources, and
the PSF- and noise-aware variants by a further 0.1--0.2\% relative to
the control, with intervals including zero. The PSF and noise
variant reduces fixed-moment shape RMSE by 1.0\% with an interval
excluding zero, but none of the continuations clearly improves
adaptive shapes. We therefore retain the original ResNet as the main
model for the remainder of the paper. ResNet-D5 has slightly lower
source RMSE and comparable shape performance, and we keep it as a
comparator.

\section{\label{sc:discussion}Discussion and summary}

The residual networks achieve lower image errors and stronger
spatial correlation with the observed JWST reference, while
diffusion retains more small-scale power (Fig.~\ref{fig:pixel_comp}).
Apparent sharpness is therefore an incomplete measure of
reconstruction quality: a prediction can contain fine-scale
variation without placing it where it appears in the reference. This
is the expected behaviour of the two model families described in
Sect.~\ref{sc:methods}: a generative model draws one plausible image
from the set consistent with the input, whereas a deterministic
model trained on absolute error returns a central estimate that
omits detail the input does not constrain. For the measurements
tested here, accuracy pixel by pixel is what matters, and the
residual reconstructions are more accurate under every tested
metric, although their reduced power shows that some observed detail
is smoothed away. The diffusion model retains a distinct use that we
have not exploited: the spread among independent realisations gives
a per-pixel estimate of what the NISP input leaves undetermined,
which a single deterministic prediction cannot provide. Repeated
training runs and the continuation experiments support the original
ResNet as a reproducible and adequate choice; the small fixed-moment
shape gain from PSF and noise conditioning merits further study with
more varied PSFs.

The residual network is also the practical choice at survey scale.
At 0.5\,ms per galaxy on one GPU, the order $10^{8}$ galaxies of DR1
(Sect.~\ref{sc:observations}) can be reconstructed in about half a
day, whereas 50-step diffusion at 125\,ms per draw would require
several months, before any averaging over realisations. Our
cross-instrument setting is similar to that of \citet{Kahn2026}, who
translate ground-based HSC images towards HST quality with a
conditional adversarial network and report large gains in
morphological parameters; the two studies use different inputs,
targets, and metrics, and a direct comparison of architectures on a
common benchmark would be a useful next step.

The shape comparisons and controlled simulations show two useful
consequences of reconstruction: observed ellipticities agree more
closely with JWST, and compact simulated galaxies retain more
sensitivity to an applied shear. Both gains are conditional. Low-S/N
shape comparisons include only the subset of galaxies measurable in
every image, and the largest simulated galaxies at low S/N show no
response improvement. As stated in Sect.~\ref{sc:measurement}, these
are comparisons between observed images in different bands, and the
cross-band slopes are not multiplicative shear calibration factors.
Reaching the \Euclid\ requirement on multiplicative bias
\citep{Cropper2013} would require a validated treatment of
measurement and selection response across realistic morphologies,
sizes, noise, blends, and spatially varying PSFs. The sensitivity of
that calibration to the assumed galaxy morphologies
\citep{EC_ShapeDist2026} applies with particular force to a
learned reconstruction, whose output depends on the training
population as well as on the input image.

The scope of the sample also limits the conclusions. Our galaxies
lie in one COSMOS field and satisfy F814W $<25$ and
$\zph<3$, so the results do not yet demonstrate performance
on the optically fainter population that motivates near-infrared
shape measurement. The validation sample also informed model and
measurement choices. An independent evaluation on the 2876 reserved
test galaxies, followed by tests across fields and populations, is
the natural next step. Future work should also quantify residual
astrometric offsets and the effective PSFs of the input, target, and
reconstructed images, which determine how image differences
translate into morphological errors.

The improvements also motivate applications beyond cosmic shear. As
noted in Sect.~\ref{sc:intro}, the NISP bands trace rest-frame
optical light at $z\gtrsim1$, so sharper near-infrared images bear
directly on measurements of galaxy structure, merger signatures, and
blending over the Wide Survey. Residual networks have been used to
identify strong-lens candidates in the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys
\citep{Huang_et_al_2021}, and learned reconstruction has been
explored for deblending and sharpening
\citep{superres_1, Reiman_Gohre_2019, Arcelin2021, Hemmati2022, superres_2}.
Each such task would need its own labelled sample and performance
measure.

In summary, learned reconstruction improves the fidelity of NISP
imaging to the observed JWST F115W reference. Residual networks give
lower image errors and stronger spatial correlation than the tested
diffusion model, and the original ResNet reduces the observed
ellipticity-component RMSE by approximately 19\% with fixed weighted
moments and 11\% with adaptive moments. Independent training runs
reproduce its image errors, and controlled simulations show
increased shear response for compact galaxies under the NISP
effective PSFs. These results support ResNet as a practical starting
point for extracting more structural information from NISP imaging
across the \Euclid\ survey. Evaluation on the reserved test sample
and realistic simulations of instrumental, noise, and selection
effects are the next steps towards using these reconstructions for
morphological and weak lensing science.

\section*{Data and code availability}

The \Euclid\ NISP stacks used here were processed with the DR1
version of the NIR pipeline; the corresponding public products are
part of the first \Euclid\ data release \citep{DR1-TP001, DR1cite}. The
JWST images are COSMOS-Web version 0.8 mosaics; the public
COSMOS-Web data release\footnote{\url{https://cosmos2025.iap.fr/}}
\citep{Franco2025} provides the same field at the same pixel scales
with updated processing. The sample selection uses the public
COSMOS2020 Classic catalogue \citep{cosmos2020_classic}. The trained
model weights, the code for preparing the paired sample, training
the networks, and reproducing the analyses and figures, and the full
training and evaluation logs will be released publicly with
publication.
